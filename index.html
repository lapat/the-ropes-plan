The Space

Target: 3,000 sqft — meaningfully larger than 9Round's own footprint (their spec runs 1,500–2,500 sqft), so there's real room to add iPad infrastructure per station without cramping the circuit.

Criteria: New Jersey, near NYC, ideally near a train line, ideally near other gyms with an existing fight-sports following.

Recommendation: Central Avenue, Jersey City Heights

This corridor hits every criterion with real evidence behind it, not just proximity math.

  • Available now: 298 Central Ave (3,000 sqft, ~$6,000/mo) and 367 Central Ave (3,250–4,250 sqft) — both currently listed, both in range.
  • Existing fight-sports presence on the same street: Family Fitness Karate and Kickboxing runs a location at 419 Central Ave — direct evidence the corridor already supports a combat-sports business.
  • Broader Jersey City boxing/MMA scene nearby: World Boxing Gym, The Trinity Boxing Club, CULTR Fight Club, Heavy Hitters, CKO Kickboxing — a real, established market, not a cold start.
  • Transit: near the Journal Square PATH hub — not directly attached to a station, worth knowing honestly rather than oversold.

Alternative: 1 PATH Plaza has retail space literally inside a newly renovated PATH station, but the available units run larger (5,100+ sqft ground floor) — oversized for the target and further from the existing gym cluster.

Also checked: Hoboken and the waterfront. Real listings found there too (e.g. 74 Hudson St, 3,050 sqft at $78/sqft/yr), and Hoboken's transit is genuinely better — a true multimodal hub (PATH, NJ Transit, ferry, light rail all in one terminal, vs. Central Ave's HBLR-only access). But it runs roughly 2.7–3.3x the rent of Central Ave (likely $16,000–20,000/mo for a comparable space vs. $6,000/mo) and its combat-sports presence is thinner and more boutique-fitness (Rumble Boxing, CKO) rather than a real boxing/MMA cluster. Central Ave stays the pick — the rent gap is too large to justify for an unproven first location. Hoboken is worth revisiting later if this location succeeds and a second one makes sense.

Next step: this is as far as remote research can responsibly take it — touring 298/367 Central Ave with a broker is the actual next move.

Technology — Everything On the Table

Nothing here is decided. This is the real list of ideas being weighed, with an honest read on how proven or unproven each one is — not a single recommendation.

What's on the iPad at each station? Four real options

The open question that drives everything else. 9Round's own real model has no instructional screens at all — just a live trainer and a room-wide bell — which is the honest baseline to compare every idea below against. None of these are picked yet:

  1. Teddy shoots fresh content on a cadence. New videos weekly or monthly — real courses, Teddy just talking into a camera about what he's teaching that cycle. Teddy Jr then improvises off that material live on the floor, so it's always current, not one static library recorded once and never updated.
  2. Teddy's voice plus video, for technique specifically. An AI version of him — his real voice, possibly his face — walking someone through technique at a station. Unproven, may not work well. The voice (cloned from his own audio) is realistic with existing tools; the video/talking-face part is a harder, separate step that can look uncanny — worth prototyping and actually testing before betting on it.
  3. AI punch tracking. A live counter/gamified score from the camera. This part is solved and already working — see below.
  4. No app at all. Keep version one simple — pure 9Round model, screen adds nothing, all the teaching comes from Teddy Jr live on the floor.

Computer vision — what a camera can actually do

Researched in real depth, tested against real footage. Two very different pieces:

  • Punch counting — solved, achievable now. A working prototype already counts punches from real bag-work video using Apple's own on-device camera technology. No custom hardware needed.
  • Real technique coaching ("your chin's up," "rotate your hips") — a genuine, unsolved research problem. Even a $5.8M-funded company with a dedicated computer-vision team hasn't shipped this yet. Possible eventually, not a version-one bet.

Sensors and wearables

Custom bag or glove sensors (gamified punch data, à la FightCamp) are real hardware — something that has to be built, tested, and can break or feel janky, which is a genuine cost most software ideas don't carry. Off-the-shelf heart-rate wearables (the MYZONE approach 9Round and Orangetheory actually use) are safer — mature, proven, nothing to build.

Testing It First — Pop-Up Clinics

Before signing any lease: run a pop-up clinic at a gym Teddy already has real ties to, to build buzz and prove demand.

Gleason's Gym — the strongest real lead

Brooklyn (DUMBO), the oldest active boxing gym in the US, since 1937. This isn't a cold outreach target — Teddy has a genuine, decades-documented history there, training fighters under the Cus D'Amato lineage going back to the 1980s. Gleason's also already runs a recurring guest-clinic program (Personal Trainer's Clinic, All Female Boxing Clinic, Masters Sparring Clinic) in a proven weekend format — 2 days, ~8 classes, priced as a package rather than hourly rental.

Typical cost for a name-trainer weekend clinic runs roughly $400–500/attendee, or hourly space rental elsewhere in NYC runs $250–1,000+/hr for name-brand gyms. A 6–12 week pre-sale waitlist campaign (100–150 "founding members" at a discount) is standard practice for validating real demand before committing to a lease.

Legal & Social Media Prep

Prep work that can start before the space is even secured, per the "sooner the better" priority.

Decisions needed from Jay first

  • Is the $250K a capital contribution (Jay/Louis get equity in a jointly-owned LLC) or a loan/gift to Teddy and Teddy Jr (no equity, they own it outright)? This decides the entire legal structure underneath it.
  • Whose name is the business licensed and insured under — the LLC, or Teddy/Teddy Jr personally?

Execution once that's settled

  • Form the NJ business entity — a direct New Jersey LLC ($125 filing fee) is simpler and cheaper than routing through an Illinois parent entity plus NJ foreign qualification, unless there's a specific reason to fold this into an existing Illinois corporate structure (that'd cost roughly double in ongoing filings — worth asking why before defaulting to it)
  • An operating/partnership agreement spelling out Teddy Jr's management role and pay, and Jay/Louis's role
  • A written name/likeness agreement for using Teddy's name and brand commercially — in writing, not a handshake, since the whole venture's value rides on his name, and doubly true now that "The Ropes" turns out to already be his own product
  • Standard boxing-gym liability waiver plus an assault/contact-sport insurance rider — New Jersey courts do generally uphold these (Stelluti v. Casapenn, 2011) for ordinary negligence, but not for gross negligence or coaching/supervision failures, so it needs to be drafted for a real contact-sport gym specifically, not a generic template
  • Lease review once a Central Ave space is chosen
  • Confirm "The Ropes" name usage directly with Teddy — not really a "trademark check" anymore since we now know he already owns it

NJ Health Club Services Act — a real requirement, not optional

New Jersey has a specific law (N.J.S.A. 56:8-39) covering any business devoting 40%+ of its space to fitness services — this gym qualifies. It requires registering with the NJ Division of Consumer Affairs before opening, and if memberships run longer than 3 months, posting a surety bond (10% of prior-year gross income, $25K–$50K floor/ceiling). Members also get a mandatory 3-day cancellation right. A month-to-month membership model with no long-term contracts sidesteps the bonding requirement — worth deciding the membership structure with this in mind, not as an afterthought.

Jersey City also requires a separate local Commerce Division business license beyond state registration, plus a Certificate of Occupancy for the fit-out.

The $150K / $100K math — real numbers, and it's tight

If Teddy Jr takes $150K of the $250K as salary, that leaves $100K for rent and legal before opening. Running the actual numbers against the Central Ave space (~$6,000/mo):

  • 12 months' rent: $72,000
  • Security deposit (commercial landlords often want 3+ months from a new operator, more for a first-time contact-sport tenant): $18,000–36,000
  • LLC formation + attorney (operating agreement, licensing agreement, real liability waiver): ~$3,500
  • Insurance (general liability + contact-sport rider): ~$3,000

Total: $96,500–114,500 — before a single dollar of buildout (flooring, bags, mats, iPads, signage), which is a separate five-figure cost on a 3,000 sqft space. Honest read: $100K is not enough once buildout is counted. Either the salary/budget split needs to shift, more capital needs to come in, or lease terms need to land meaningfully better than these base-case numbers.

Social media — what Teddy already has, and non-obvious ways to use it

His real reach right now: ~332K YouTube subscribers ("THE FIGHT with Teddy Atlas," 1,800+ videos), ~205K Instagram, ~76.5K X/Twitter. His ~30-year run as ESPN's boxing analyst ended in November 2025 — he's now fully independent, which actually strengthens the pitch: no network exclusivity, and real incentive to build his own thing harder than ever. His audience skews national, not local — mostly not geographically near wherever the gym lands, which matters for how it gets used.

Existing asset worth knowing about: he already sells a $97 structured boxing instructional product ("Fundamentals of Boxing") and, per the flag at the top of this page, already runs the teddyatlasboxing.com "The Ropes" app with tutorials, community, and paid coaching tiers.

  • Mine the archive. 1,800 YouTube videos plus 30 years of ESPN commentary is a huge amount of real teaching content already sitting there. AI transcription + classification can pull out standalone teachable moments automatically — turns a filming bottleneck into a curation problem that's already mostly solved.
  • Build a "coaching philosophy" reference document from his own words. Transcribe and analyze his real interviews so any AI-assisted copywriting or in-app content actually sounds like him, grounded in what he's really said — not invented.
  • Use his non-local following for the pop-up clinic, not just the gym. Since most of his audience isn't near NJ, a pop-up at Gleason's (see above) reaches people who'd never walk into the physical gym but will show up for a one-time event — different audience, different job.
  • Turn the ESPN exit into the actual launch story. "30 years commentating, now it's personal" is a real, timely hook tied to a real recent event, not generic gym marketing.
  • Let AI do a first pass on his paid video evaluations (already a feature on his existing app) — his time is the bottleneck on that premium tier; AI flagging the priority issues first lets his limited hours go further.
